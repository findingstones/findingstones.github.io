There is a certain sweetness in imagining idealised versions of yourself. Versions that embody your aspirations and inhabit a world moulded to their potential.

You might be lying on the sofa, splitting what remains of your attention between these imagined lives and the glowing stupor of a screen. Somewhere in the back of your mind, you know you should be doing something else. Anything besides rotting away through indistinguishable days.

But the sofa is too comfy. Work has punched you in the chin and left you groggy. The screen is a blanket wrapped around your brain.

Perhaps you imagine yourself as a musician. Not just a dimwit robot who plays fast, but someone who is able to purposefully craft songs that move audiences. Perhaps you imagine being financially independent, free from the shackles of employment and entrepreneurship, with the time and freedom to pursue whatever catches your curiosity. Or perhaps you see yourself as a writer, traveller, a role model, or even a hero in whatever path you are interested in.

These imagined identities are exciting. They open a door and offer a glimpse of what you think life could be.

But why these identities? Why not anything else?

It might be that they emerge as attempts to satisfy our psychological needs. The desire for mastery, autonomy, connection, or novelty. These needs shape what we find meaningful and, consequently, the goals we pursue.

It is important to mention that goals are not quite the same as identities. Setting a goal of learning to play an instrument is not the same as the identity of being a musician who can express himself through music. Deciding to go to the gym is a goal. Having your desired body and interacting with others with the confidence given by your new body is an identity.

I don't think we mostly fantasise about completing the stepping stones required to achieve our goals. No matter how many daily incantations of "the journey is the destination" we try. We imagine the possibilities that completing our goals would unlock. We dream about living a life in which something important to us is finally being satisfied.

Our imagined identities are us living with the consequences of completing our goals.

I have multiple imagined identities. I can see myself as someone who has mastered multiple languages, a writer of multiple books, a researcher who impacts different areas of knowledge that catch my attention, a marathon runner, a cyclist travelling around the world, and much more.

Thinking about them feels great.

Have I done anything to become these identities?

Of course not!

But imagining them can feel as if I am savouring the rewards and satisfaction associated with the person I could become, without having to do any of the work required to get there.

It is the end of the road, a road which happens to be pristine.

There are no setbacks. No rejection. No embarrassing attempts. No tedious repetition. No slow grind after work. The imagined writer does not struggle for words. The imagined musician does not freeze in front of an audience. The imagined wealthy person has forgotten what it took to become wealthy.

Everything has been accomplished. The goal is finished. The capabilities are there. Possibilities unlocked. And life bends around them.

The imagined identities give you a taste of the rewards while removing the process. They are an emotional advance payment on an achievement not earned.

These identities can be dangerous.

Because no matter how sweet these imagined lives may be, there is still your current reality, constantly hitting you in the face with a brick.

The distance between your present self and the people inhabiting your fantasies is huge. Every day that passes without meaningful progress is a testament to your failure and weakness.

And you still don't move.

Excuses might come up here and there, but deep down you know that you could have done at least something. Or if you did something, you know it wasn't a real effort. You could have done a bit more.

Slowly, the identities that once inspired you become a source of self-reproach. "Look what you could become" transforms into "Look what you have failed to become". What began as an expression of hope turns into another standard against which to measure yourself. And you are failing. And your mark of shame grows.

And you still don't move.

Because abandoning these identities is not as simple as abandoning a fantasy. Behind them are genuine desires, genuine psychological needs that your current life does not satisfy.

So the fantasies remain. They comfort you by offering glimpses of a sanitised existence, and hurt you by exposing the distance between that existence and your imperfect one. Caught between the pleasure of imagining and the pain of comparison.

And you plough along with your day.

Wage slaving 9 to 5. Monday to Friday. Daydreaming. Never chasing.

The identities we construct to give our lives direction can keep us exactly where we are.

And thus we remain hypothetical.
